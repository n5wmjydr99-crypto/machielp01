# Legal and platform rules for synthetic/cloned voices in monetized short videos (YouTube Shorts, Instagram Reels, TikTok): status as of November 2026

_Method note: this session's network policy blocked direct fetches of support.google.com, support.tiktok.com and coqui.ai. Re-checking against the live pages requires allowing those hosts in the session's network settings. The policy wording marked as quoted below comes from search-engine extracts of the official pages, from official blogs, or from reputable press, and should be checked against the live pages before it is relied on. Creator context: two animated (3D) characters voiced with TTS, a creator based in Argentina, Paraguay or Uruguay, and an audience across Latin America. This is general information, not legal advice._

## 1. Platform policies: AI-voice labels and monetization eligibility (YouTube, Instagram/Meta, TikTok)

### Takeaway
None of the three platforms bans TTS or AI voices, and none makes an AI-voiced channel ineligible just for using them. Required AI labels apply to *realistic* content: realistic people or scenes, or a real person's voice. YouTube explicitly exempts clearly animated characters voiced with a non-cloned TTS voice. Meta ("realistic-sounding audio") and TikTok ("realistic-looking scenes or people") leave this case as a gray area. The real monetization risks are different:
- **Low-originality rules.** YouTube's "inauthentic content" rule covers mass-produced, templated videos. It was renamed in July 2025 and clarified in July 2026. TikTok and Meta have their own originality rules.
- **Geography.** TikTok's Creator Rewards Program and Instagram's direct Reels payouts do not appear to be available in Argentina, Paraguay or Uruguay.

### Cited Findings

#### YouTube: altered/synthetic ("GenAI") content disclosure
- The help article "Disclosing use of altered or synthetic content" now appears in some locales as "Disclosing use of GenAI content" — [YouTube Help 14328491](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en-GB&co=GENIE.Platform%3DDesktop)
- The original rule (March 2024) requires disclosure of realistic altered or synthetic content. YouTube's examples include using the likeness of a realistic person, such as synthetically generating a person's voice to narrate a video — [YouTube Blog, "How we're helping creators disclose altered or synthetic content" (Mar 2024)](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/disclosing-ai-generated-content/)
- Animation exemption, 2026 wording: "non-realistic content, such as animations or fantastical scenes, also doesn't require a disclosure" — [YouTube Community post, "Updates to AI content disclosure and labels" (≈May 2026)](https://support.google.com/youtube/blog/424874071/updates-to-ai-content-disclosure-and-labels?hl=en)
- The help page also exempts cloning one's *own* voice to make voiceovers or dubs (per a search extract) — [YouTube Help 14328491](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)
- Where the label appears for non-realistic material: "For content that is unrealistic, animated, or slightly altered, viewers can find this disclosure in the expanded description." (extract from YouTube's 2026 label-update material) — [YouTube Community update](https://support.google.com/youtube/blog/424874071/updates-to-ai-content-disclosure-and-labels?hl=en)
- May 2026 change: "if a creator doesn't specify whether or not they used AI, but our systems detect significant photorealistic AI use, we will now automatically apply a label." Realistic AI content now gets one prominent label format, and creators can dispute a wrong label in Studio — [YouTube Blog, "Improving AI labels for viewers and creators" (May 2026)](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/improving-ai-labels-viewers-creators/)
- Reach: YouTube's Rene Ritchie is reported as saying labels "alone do not affect how our videos are recommended". This is a single secondary source — [air.io](https://air.io/en/youtube-hacks/youtube-ai-disclosure-in-2026-label-it-yourself-or-youtube-will)
- Penalties (search extract of YouTube's help/blog wording): "Creators who consistently choose not to disclose this information may be subject to manual application of a label, or penalties from YouTube, including removal of content or suspension from the YouTube Partner Program." — [YouTube Help 14328491](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en); [YouTube Blog (Mar 2024)](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/disclosing-ai-generated-content/)
- Privacy route for cloned voices: since mid-2024, people can ask YouTube to remove AI content that simulates their face or voice. YouTube weighs whether the content is labeled as synthetic, whether it uniquely identifies the person, and whether it is parody or satire. Filing a request does not guarantee removal — [Engadget (Jul 2024)](https://www.engadget.com/new-youtube-policy-lets-you-request-the-removal-of-ai-generated-content-that-uses-your-likeness-211522438.html)
- YouTube's "likeness detection" tool was opened to all adults (18+, selfie + ID) in 2026. It only detects faces, not AI-cloned voices, so the privacy complaint remains the route for voices — [Android Authority](https://www.androidauthority.com/youtube-likeness-detection-expansion-3667840/); [Malwarebytes (May 2026)](https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/ai/2026/05/youtube-wants-your-face-to-fight-deepfakes)

  2. "unsatisfying or off-putting" or distressing content;

- Account-level label (2026): Instagram launched an optional "AI creator" profile label in May 2026 — [MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/414862/instagram-unveils-new-ai-creator-account-label.html). ### Inferences
- **Labels for this specific case** (animated characters plus generic TTS voices *not* cloned from a real person):
  - **YouTube:** no required disclosure, because of the explicit animation exemption. Disclosure becomes required if a character's voice imitates or clones a real, identifiable person, since that is "likeness of a realistic person".
  - **Meta:** the trigger "realistic-sounding audio that was digitally created" could be read to cover a very natural TTS voice even on a cartoon. Turning on the per-post "AI info" label is the low-risk choice; no source documents a reach penalty for the label itself, and penalties attach to *non*-disclosure. The "AI-generated profile" account label targets AI-generated *people*. A clearly cartoon account seems outside it, but use it if the characters are presented as realistic humans.
  - **TikTok:** the trigger is "realistic-looking scenes or people", so cartoons are likely outside it. TikTok may still auto-label from Content Credentials, and self-labeling costs little.
- **Monetization risk comes from the format, not the voice.** A pipeline that turns out near-identical Shorts (same template, same structure, auto-written scripts) is exactly what YouTube's "generic, repetitive or template-based" group targets. The characters should also never act as "experts" on health, finance, legal or political topics.
- **YouTube is the main revenue option.** For a creator in Argentina, Paraguay or Uruguay, YPP is the only one of the three programs with documented ad revenue available locally (Argentina and Uruguay confirmed, Paraguay not). TikTok's CRP appears unavailable in all three countries. Instagram's direct payouts appear limited to certain markets. Revenue on TikTok and Instagram would likely come from brand deals or other tools.
- **Video length on TikTok.** CRP has been widely reported to pay only for videos longer than 1 minute; this was not verified. If it still applies, Shorts-length TikToks (<60 s) would not earn CRP even where the program exists.

## 2. Licenses: what TTS model and dataset licenses impose on generated audio

### Takeaway
The license on the **model weights**, not the code, decides whether the audio can be monetized.
- **Apache-2.0 or MIT weights:** in practice these place no conditions on generated audio. Their conditions govern redistributing the software or weights. This is an inference; I found no source that says it outright.
- **Non-commercial weights rule out monetized videos:**
  - XTTS-v2 is under the Coqui Public Model License (CPML), which "allows only non-commercial use of a machine learning model and its outputs". Coqui shut down in January 2024, so no commercial license can be bought.
  - The official F5-TTS weights are CC-BY-NC because they were trained on the Emilia dataset.
  - Monetized videos are commercial use, so neither model should voice them.
- **CC BY-SA 4.0 datasets** (Google's OpenSLR Latin American Spanish sets): it is not settled whether they bind synthesized audio.
  - CC's own guidance says ShareAlike (SA) applies if outputs are "based on" SA content and shared publicly.
  - The legal code treats any sound recording synced to moving images as an "adaptation".
  - CC licenses never license the speakers' personality rights.

  The cautious path is to give attribution and not to clone any one identifiable volunteer.

### Cited Findings
- XTTS-v2 / CPML:
  - "This license allows only non-commercial use of a machine learning model and its outputs." — [CPML 1.0.0 text (Hugging Face copy)](https://huggingface.co/coqui/XTTS-v1/raw/main/LICENSE)
  - The XTTS-v2 model card says "This model is licensed under Coqui Public Model License" — [coqui/XTTS-v2 model card](https://huggingface.co/coqui/XTTS-v2/blob/v2.0.1/README.md?code=true)
  - The CPML also states: "Use of the model to train other models for commercial use is not a non-commercial purpose." — [CPML text (HF)](https://huggingface.co/coqui/XTTS-v1/raw/main/LICENSE)
- Coqui Inc. shut down in January 2024, so "there is no longer anyone to sell you a commercial license". The Coqui TTS *library* is MPL-2.0 (commercial use allowed), and running a different, permissively licensed model through it does not carry the CPML restriction — [LocalAIMaster (secondary)](https://localaimaster.com/blog/xtts-coqui-commercial-license)
- F5-TTS: "Our code is released under MIT License. The pre-trained models are licensed under the CC-BY-NC license due to the training data Emilia" — [F5-TTS README (HF mirror)](https://huggingface.co/spaces/mrfakename/E2-F5-TTS/blob/a576047264583b9c09f6ecb469e0144f41fb3136/README_REPO.md?code=true). The checkpoint license was changed to CC-BY-NC-4.0 "following the used training set Emilia" — [SWivid/F5-TTS commit](https://huggingface.co/SWivid/F5-TTS/commit/0cce16ebafcdfabda8a9c4f53c323e240f296cd7)
- Emilia: the original Emilia set (101k hours) is CC BY-NC 4.0; Emilia-YODAS (114k hours) is CC BY 4.0 — [Emilia dataset card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/amphion/Emilia-Dataset). Maintainers commented that using *only* Emilia-YODAS "should be fine for commercial use"; this is a forum comment, not a legal opinion — [Emilia discussion #13](https://huggingface.co/datasets/amphion/Emilia-Dataset/discussions/13)
- Code and weights licenses can differ:
  - OmniVoice was published as Apache-2.0 and relicensed to CC-BY-NC after a user pointed out it was trained on Emilia — [OmniVoice discussion #29](https://huggingface.co/k2-fsa/OmniVoice/discussions/29)
  - OpenF5-TTS Base is listed as Apache-2.0, trained only on Emilia-YODAS; it is English-only, alpha-quality and weaker than the official NC model — [OpenF5-TTS-Base card](https://huggingface.co/mrfakename/OpenF5-TTS-Base)
  - OpenF5-Intermediate is CC-BY 4.0 but still produces low-quality speech — [OpenF5-Intermediate](https://huggingface.co/mrfakename/OpenF5-Intermediate)
- CC NonCommercial definition (license legal code): "NonCommercial means not primarily intended for or directed towards commercial advantage or monetary compensation." — [CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 legal code](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-sa/4.0/legalcode.en)
- CC on NonCommercial data in AI: "following the NC restriction would require that all stages, from copying the data during training to sharing the trained model, must not be for commercial gain" — [Creative Commons, "Using CC-licensed works for AI training" (2025)](https://creativecommons.org/using-cc-licensed-works-for-ai-training-2/)
- CC on training generally: there are "many scenarios where compliance with CC license conditions is not required when using CC-licensed works for AI training". Where copyright exceptions or limitations apply, the license terms don't apply. Attribution for training "could be minimal", e.g. a link to the dataset source — [CC 2025 FAQ](https://creativecommons.org/using-cc-licensed-works-for-ai-training-2/); [PDF version](https://creativecommons.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Using-CC-licensed-Works-for-AI-Training.pdf)
- CC on ShareAlike: "if AI models or outputs are based on ShareAlike content and they will be shared publicly, following the ShareAlike condition would require AI developers to use the same CC license as the original works". The Attribution (BY) and ShareAlike (SA) conditions are triggered only when works or adaptations are publicly shared — [CC 2025 FAQ](https://creativecommons.org/using-cc-licensed-works-for-ai-training-2/)
- CC notes "significant legal uncertainty" about whether copyright applies to AI training — [CC legal primer (15 May 2025)](https://creativecommons.org/2025/05/15/understanding-cc-licenses-and-ai-training-a-legal-primer/). Its 2023 guidance says the CC license a creator chooses applies to the creative work the creator contributes, even if the AI-generated portion is uncopyrightable — [CC (Aug 2023)](https://creativecommons.org/2023/08/18/understanding-cc-licenses-and-generative-ai/)
- CC BY-SA 4.0 legal code, two key clauses — [CC BY-SA 4.0 legal code](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/legalcode.en):
  - (a) "For purposes of this Public License, where the Licensed Material is a musical work, performance, or sound recording, Adapted Material is always produced where the Licensed Material is synched in timed relation with a moving image."
  - (b) "Moral rights, such as the right of integrity, are not licensed under this Public License, nor are publicity, privacy, and/or other similar personality rights". The licensor waives only *its own* such rights, and only to the limited extent needed.
- OpenSLR SLR61 ("Crowdsourced high-quality Argentinian Spanish speech data set"):
  - 5,739 recordings of native speakers, made by volunteers in Buenos Aires; manually quality-checked "but there might still be errors"; copyright 2018–2019 Google; license CC BY-SA 4.0. Users should cite Guevara-Rukoz et al., LREC 2020 — [OpenSLR 61](https://www.openslr.org/61/); [about.html](https://openslr.org/resources/61/about.html); [HF OpenSLR card (cc-by-sa-4.0)](https://huggingface.co/datasets/openslr/blob/main/README.md); [LREC 2020 paper](https://aclanthology.org/2020.lrec-1.801); [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/crowdsourcing-latin-american-spanish-for-low-resource-text-to-speech/)
  - The paper's PDF carries a CC-BY-NC notice. That applies to the paper, not to the dataset — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2020.lrec-1.801)

### Inferences
- **Apache-2.0 / MIT weights.** These licenses' conditions apply when you redistribute the software or weights: keep notices, include the license, and (Apache) state your changes. Generated speech is not normally a "Derivative Work" of the weights. Audio from truly Apache- or MIT-licensed *weights* can therefore be monetized without attribution, though crediting is good practice. Caveat: the license on the GitHub repo often covers only the code. F5-TTS (MIT code, NC weights) and OmniVoice (Apache label later corrected to NC) show that you must check (1) the weights' license on the model card and (2) the training datasets.
- **Non-commercial weights** (CPML, CC BY-NC, Emilia-trained). Monetized Shorts, Reels or TikToks, and sponsored posts, are "directed towards … monetary compensation", so audio generated with XTTS-v2 or the official F5-TTS weights should not appear in them. XTTS-v2 has no licensing path because Coqui has closed. Spanish fine-tunes of these checkpoints most likely inherit the restriction (unverified; see Gaps). Turning monetization on later would not make earlier NC-generated audio compliant.
- **CC BY-SA 4.0 data** (OpenSLR 61, and the sister LatAm Spanish sets, assumed to be under the same CC BY-SA terms but not checked here):
  - (a) Training or fine-tuning may not trigger the license at all where a copyright exception applies. Whether such exceptions exist in Argentina, Paraguay or Uruguay was not researched.
  - (b) If you **publish the fine-tuned model**, CC's guidance points to licensing it CC BY-SA 4.0, with attribution.
  - (c) For **generated audio in a video**, CC's guidance says SA would apply if outputs are "based on" SA content and shared publicly. If the speech counted as an adaptation of the recordings, clause (a) above would make the *whole video* an adaptation. The video would then have to be CC BY-SA, and anyone could legally re-upload it.
  - Whether model output that does not reproduce the recordings is an "adaptation" is unresolved, so the practical risk is low to moderate.
  - Cheap mitigations: put an attribution line in the description; never put raw dataset clips in a video; don't use a single volunteer's clip as the reference for zero-shot cloning. If you need full exclusivity over the videos, use voices from permissive data (CC0/CC BY) or your own recordings.
- **Personality rights are separate.** Because CC licenses expressly exclude personality and publicity rights, the dataset license does not authorize turning one identifiable volunteer's voice into a commercial character (see Section 3).

### Gaps
- I found no primary or law-firm source in this session that says explicitly that Apache-2.0 or MIT impose no obligations on model outputs. The statement above is an inference from how those licenses are structured.
- The full CPML text (definitions of "Output" and "Non-Commercial Purposes") was not retrieved from coqui.ai, which was blocked. I relied on the Hugging Face copy as extracted by search.
- A third-party guide quotes the F5-TTS maintainer (GitHub Discussion #997) as saying the NC restriction "survives fine-tuning". I did not verify this first-hand.
- I found no court decision or official CC statement on whether speech from a model fine-tuned on BY-SA recordings counts as "Adapted Material".
- I could not find the consent terms the OpenSLR Latin American Spanish volunteers signed, so it is unknown whether they cover commercial voice cloning.
- The licenses of other specific Spanish-capable models and voices (e.g., Piper voices, Kokoro, Chatterbox) were not checked here.

## 3. Voice and personality rights: cloning real voices without consent (Argentina, Paraguay, Uruguay, Mexico, Spain) and 2024–2026 AI voice laws

### Takeaway
In every target jurisdiction, a person's voice is protected, or soon will be, as a personality attribute that needs consent for commercial reproduction.
- **Argentina:** art. 53 of the Civil and Commercial Code (CCyC) already names "la voz" and requires consent to reproduce it "de cualquier modo".
- **Mexico:** a reform of the federal copyright law (LFDA) was published in the official gazette (DOF) on 14 May 2026. It protects performers' voices (and their characters) against AI cloning, with heavy fines.
- **Spain:** Organic Law 1/1982 already treats commercial use of someone's voice without consent as an unlawful intrusion, and a 2026 bill would explicitly cover AI-simulated voices.
- **Uruguay and Paraguay:** they rely on general constitutional, civil and data-protection rules; only bills are pending.
- **Elsewhere:** Tennessee's ELVIS Act is in force; the US federal NO FAKES Act and Denmark's deepfake bill are still pending. The EU AI Act's Art. 50 transparency duties apply from 2 Aug 2026.

Cloning one dataset volunteer's recognizable voice for a commercial character is legally risky. A generic synthetic voice that doesn't resemble anyone is low-risk.

### Cited Findings

#### Argentina (law in force + pending bills)

  - a priority scientific, cultural or educational interest, with precautions to avoid unnecessary harm;
  - the regular exercise of the right to report on matters of general interest.

#### EU AI Act Article 50 (relevant to Spain and EU audiences)
- **Definition (Art. 3(60)):** a "deep fake" is AI-generated or manipulated image, audio or video content that "resembles existing persons, objects, places, entities or events" and would falsely appear authentic or truthful — [European Commission FAQ on Art. 50](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act); [EP adopted text](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/TA-9-2024-0138_EN.pdf)
- **Deployer duty (Art. 50(4)):** deployers must disclose deepfakes, and cannot rely only on the provider's machine-readable marking under Art. 50(2).
  - **Light-touch case:** for "evidently artistic, creative, satirical, fictional" works, the obligation "is limited to the disclosure of the deepfake content in an appropriate manner that does not hamper the display or enjoyment of the work".
  - **Personal use:** purely personal, non-professional use is outside the Act.

  Source: [Commission FAQ](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act); [AI Act guide](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/transparency-rules-article-50/)
- **Timing:** Art. 50 has applied since 2 August 2026 and was **not** postponed.
  - The Digital Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744) took effect on 27 July 2026. It delayed the Annex III high-risk obligations to 2 Dec 2027.
  - It only added a grace period until 2 Dec 2026 on Art. 50(2) marking for generative systems already on the market before 2 Aug 2026.

  Sources: [Usercentrics](https://usercentrics.com/knowledge-hub/eu-ai-act-high-risk-delay-article-50-transparency-consent/); [ActuIA](https://www.actuia.com/en/news/ai-act-obligations-in-force-and-delays-under-omnibus-2026-1744/); [Technology.org (13 Oct 2026)](https://www.technology.org/2026/10/13/eu-ai-act-deadlines-after-the-digital-omnibus-what-applies-from-december-2026-to-august-2028/); [Elevate Consult](https://elevateconsult.com/insights/eu-ai-act-article-50-icp-impact/)

#### Dataset volunteers
- The SLR61 recordings were made by volunteers in Buenos Aires and licensed by Google under CC BY-SA 4.0 — [OpenSLR 61](https://www.openslr.org/61/). CC 4.0 licenses do not license "publicity, privacy, and/or other similar personality rights" — [CC BY-SA 4.0 legal code](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/legalcode.en)

### Inferences
- **Generic synthetic voices.** A TTS voice that doesn't resemble an identifiable person has no personality-right holder. Even in the EU it is arguably not a "deep fake", because it does not resemble an existing person. This is the lowest-risk option.
- **Cloned or soundalike voices.** Cloning or imitating a real person's voice without written consent is the highest-risk option.
  - **Argentina:** it directly engages art. 53 CCyC ("reproducir … la voz … de cualquier modo").
  - **Mexico:** for performers and dubbing actors (a very common target in Latin American content), it is now an explicit violation of arts. 87 and 118 VII LFDA with six- or seven-figure peso fines. The fine range matches 5,000–40,000 UMA, consistent with El Imparcial's "hasta 40 mil días de salario".
  - **Spain:** commercial use of someone's voice is already an intrusion under Organic Law 1/1982.
  - Because the audience spans Latin America, a claim could arise where the person lives (an inference; jurisdiction not researched).
- **Dataset volunteers.** Using a dataset volunteer's voice for a commercial character is risky if the voice stays recognizable. That happens with single-speaker fine-tunes or zero-shot cloning from that person's clips.
  - The CC license is not consent for personality-right uses.
  - The volunteers are presumably Argentine (recorded in Buenos Aires), so art. 53 applies at home.
  - The risk falls if the model blends many speakers into a voice that identifies no one.
  - Scale matters. A recognizable voice in a few videos has a low chance of being noticed, but monetized, recurring characters raise both the exposure and the damages.
- **Disclosure helps under future rules.** The EU AI Act's light-touch rule for fictional works, Spain's proposed fiction exception (public must be able to tell AI was used) and YouTube's privacy review (it weighs whether content is labeled) all favor a plain "voces generadas con IA" disclosure, even when platforms don't require it.
- **EU reach.** The AI Act covers deployers outside the EU when the output is used in the EU (Art. 2(1)(c); not re-verified in this session). A monetizing creator is acting professionally, so the personal-use exclusion likely doesn't apply if EU/Spanish viewers are targeted.

## 4. Practical checklist for staying safe

### Takeaway
A creator voicing two animated characters can monetize safely by doing six things:
1. Using only TTS weights whose license allows commercial use.
2. Keeping a license dossier for each voice.
3. Crediting any CC datasets in each video description.
4. Never cloning or imitating a real, identifiable person (celebrities, dubbing actors, dataset volunteers) without a written release.
5. Disclosing AI voices in plain language and using the platform toggles when the audio sounds realistic.
6. Keeping each video clearly original, not templated mass production.

### Cited Findings
- XTTS-v2 (CPML) allows "only non-commercial use of a machine learning model and its outputs" — [CPML (HF)](https://huggingface.co/coqui/XTTS-v1/raw/main/LICENSE). The official F5-TTS weights are CC-BY-NC because of Emilia — [F5-TTS README](https://huggingface.co/spaces/mrfakename/E2-F5-TTS/blob/a576047264583b9c09f6ecb469e0144f41fb3136/README_REPO.md?code=true). Repo or code licenses can differ from weights licenses — [OmniVoice #29](https://huggingface.co/k2-fsa/OmniVoice/discussions/29)
- CC's guidance on training says attribution "could be minimal", e.g. a link to the dataset source, and that SA applies to publicly shared outputs "based on" SA content — [CC 2025 FAQ](https://creativecommons.org/using-cc-licensed-works-for-ai-training-2/). SLR61 asks users to cite the LREC 2020 paper — [OpenSLR 61](https://www.openslr.org/61/)
- Consent rules for voice: Argentina, art. 53 CCyC — [abogados.com.ar](https://abogados.com.ar/la-imagen-y-la-voz-frente-a-la-inteligencia-artificial-generativa-a-la-luz-de-la-enciclica-magnica-humanitas/39391); Mexico, arts. 87 and 118 VII LFDA — [Galicia](https://galicia.com.mx/links/files/Actualizaciones/GC.-Actualizacion.-TMT.-Reforma-LFT-y-Derecho-de-Autor-en-materia-de-IA.pdf); Spain, Organic Law 1/1982 art. 7.6 — [Pintos & Salgado](https://pintos-salgado.com/deepfakes-vs-derecho-a-la-imagen-y-la-voz/)
- Platform triggers:
  - YouTube: animation exempt from disclosure — [YouTube Community](https://support.google.com/youtube/blog/424874071/updates-to-ai-content-disclosure-and-labels?hl=en); auto-labels since May 2026 — [YouTube Blog](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/improving-ai-labels-viewers-creators/)
  - Meta: "realistic-sounding audio" — [Meta](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/02/labeling-ai-generated-images-on-facebook-instagram-and-threads/)
  - TikTok: "realistic-looking scenes or people", plus C2PA auto-labels — [TikTok](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines/en/integrity-authenticity)
- Monetization: YouTube's inauthentic-content groups (template-based content; AI personas on sensitive topics) — [Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2026/07/13/youtube-inauthentic-content-monetization-policy-update/); TikTok's originality requirement — [CRP Terms (EEA)](https://www.tiktok.com/legal/page/global/tiktok-creator-rewards-program-eea/en); YouTube's 30-day fix-it period and mistaken AI flags — [Tubefilter (Nov 2026)](https://www.tubefilter.com/2026/11/03/youtube-spam-policy-content-violation-grace-period-appeals/)
- EU fictional-work disclosure "in an appropriate manner that does not hamper the display or enjoyment of the work" — [Commission FAQ](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act)

### Inferences
1. **Pick models by weights license.**
   - OK for monetized videos: weights under Apache-2.0, MIT, CC BY 4.0 or CC0, with training data compatible with commercial use. Read the model card, not only the GitHub LICENSE.
   - Not OK: CPML (XTTS-v2), CC BY-NC or BY-NC-SA, "research only" licenses, Emilia-trained checkpoints (unless trained only on Emilia-YODAS), and fine-tunes of any of these.
2. **Keep a license dossier per character voice**, e.g. a `licencias/` folder. Include:
   - model name, version or commit hash, download URL and date;
   - copies of the LICENSE file and model card (saved as PDF or screenshot);
   - the licenses of all datasets used;
   - for any reference or cloning audio: where it came from, plus the signed release;
   - the generation settings (speaker ID, seed).

   Keep it for as long as the channel is monetized. It also supports appeals.
3. **Credit CC data in each video description** (and in the channel's About section), e.g. «Voces sintéticas generadas con [modelo] ([licencia]). Datos de entrenamiento: "Crowdsourced high-quality Argentinian Spanish speech data set", Google (OpenSLR 61), CC BY-SA 4.0, https://www.openslr.org/61/ — modificado.» Never put raw dataset recordings in a video: under BY-SA, a recording synced to video makes the video an adaptation.
4. **No soundalikes.** Don't clone or prompt toward the voice of celebrities, dubbing actors (now explicitly protected in Mexico), influencers or politicians, and don't make a recognizable clone of a single dataset volunteer. If you want a cloned voice, use your own voice or hire a voice actor with a written release covering:
   - AI synthesis and cloning;
   - commercial and monetized use;
   - the named characters;
   - platforms;
   - territory (worldwide / all of Latin America);
   - duration;
   - pay;
   - what happens if consent is withdrawn.
5. **Labels and disclosure.**
   - **YouTube:** the "altered or synthetic content" box is not required for animated characters with non-cloned voices, but is required if a voice imitates a real person. If unsure, disclose: labels reportedly don't affect recommendations.
   - **Instagram:** turn on "AI info" / "Add AI label" when the synthetic audio sounds realistic. Use "AI-generated profile" only if the account features AI-generated *people*.
   - **TikTok:** turn on the AI-generated content setting when the content is realistic. Expect auto-labels if your tools embed Content Credentials.
   - **Everywhere:** add a plain line such as «Personajes animados; voces generadas con IA». This covers EU Art. 50(4) disclosure for fictional works and Spain's proposed fiction exception, and helps in YouTube's privacy review.
6. **Avoid "inauthentic" signals.**
   - Write original scripts, and vary the stories, jokes and structure between episodes.
   - Avoid fully automated daily templated output.
   - Don't let the characters give medical, financial, legal or political advice as "experts".
   - Don't reuse other people's clips.
   - Keep project files and scripts as proof of human authorship in case of appeals.
7. **Check that each payout program is available locally** before planning revenue around it:
   - YouTube Studio → Earn: Argentina and Uruguay confirmed, Paraguay unconfirmed.
   - TikTok Studio → Creator Rewards: likely unavailable in Argentina, Paraguay and Uruguay.
   - Instagram Professional Dashboard → Monetization: availability varies by market.
8. **Re-check every 3–6 months:**
   - YouTube help pages 14328491 and 1311392;
   - Meta's Misinformation standard and AI labels;
   - TikTok's AIGC page and CRP terms;
   - Spain's Organic Law 1/1982 reform, Argentina's art. 53 bills, Paraguay's Valiente bill, and the EU Code of Practice.
9. **If someone claims a character's voice is theirs,** take the video down promptly and review the dossier. YouTube's privacy process and Mexico's fines for for-profit AI voice impersonation make delay costly.

### Gaps
- Specific Spanish-language TTS models and voices were not vetted here; this checklist assumes another research thread covers model-by-model licensing.
- No lawyer-reviewed template for a voice-actor AI release under Argentine, Paraguayan or Uruguayan law was found. The clauses listed above are a practical inference, not a validated legal template.
- How much the platforms actually penalize reach or monetization for AI labels or for non-disclosure is undocumented (Meta has published no penalty criteria; YouTube's "no effect on recommendations" claim comes from a single secondary source).
